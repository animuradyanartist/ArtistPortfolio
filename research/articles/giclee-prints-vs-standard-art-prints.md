"Art print" is one of those phrases that means everything and therefore almost nothing. It covers a two-dollar poster in a tube and a museum-grade reproduction that will outlive its buyer. When you are spending real money to put something on your wall, the word doing the work is not *print* — it is what kind.

The term you actually want to understand is **giclée**. Here is what it means, where it sits between a poster and an original, and how to tell what you are really paying for.

## What "art print" actually means

*Art print* is an umbrella, not a specification. Underneath it live very different objects:

- **Offset / poster prints** — printed in bulk on a press, in large runs, on lightweight paper. Cheap, cheerful, and not made to last decades.
- **Open-edition reproductions** — a painting photographed and reprinted, with no promise about inks, paper or colour accuracy.
- **Giclée fine-art prints** — the subject of this article: individually printed to a high standard, on archival materials, made to be kept.

Two things can both be sold as an "art print" and have almost nothing in common. So the honest question at the point of buying is never "is it a print?" — it is "what is it printed *with*, and *on*?"

## What makes a print a giclée

Giclée (pronounced *zhee-clay*, from the French for "to spray") describes a specific way of making a print, defined by three things:

- **Pigment inks, not dye.** Pigment sits on the paper as fine solid particles and resists fading; cheap dye-based inks shift and wash out over years, especially in daylight.
- **High resolution and wide colour.** A giclée is printed at high resolution from a high-quality capture of the original, so subtle transitions — the exact turn of light on water, the graduation of a sky — survive instead of banding into flat blocks.
- **Archival fine-art paper.** The print is made on heavy, acid-free art paper rather than glossy poster stock.

None of that is marketing gloss. It is the difference between a print that looks like the painting for a few years and one that still looks like it in a few decades.

## Why the paper matters more than people expect

The surface a print lives on does as much for it as the inks. My own prints are made on **archival Hahnemühle paper** — a heavyweight, acid-free cotton paper that holds pigment beautifully and does not yellow or go brittle with age. On paper like that, a landscape keeps its depth and its quiet; on thin gloss, the same image reads as a copy.

If you take one practical thing from this article, take this: **ask what paper a print is made on.** A seller who cannot answer is usually selling a poster.

## Where a giclée sits — honestly

It helps to be clear about what a giclée is *not*. It is not the original. An [original painting](/artworks) is a single, physical object — one surface the artist actually touched — and it will always be the premium, one-of-a-kind work.

A giclée is the next best relationship you can have with a painting: a faithful, lasting version that lets the image live on a wall it could otherwise never reach. It costs a fraction of an original, and — made properly — it does not feel like a compromise. It feels like the painting, printed to keep.

If you are genuinely weighing the two, I wrote a companion piece on exactly that decision: [how to choose an original painting for your space](/blog/how-to-choose-an-original-painting-for-your-space).

## How to tell what you're really buying

A short checklist for any print you are considering, mine or anyone's:

- **Inks:** pigment (archival) or dye? You want pigment.
- **Paper:** a named archival fine-art paper (e.g. Hahnemühle), or unspecified "photo paper"?
- **Edition:** is it printed to order, or run off in bulk?
- **Source:** printed from a high-resolution capture of the actual painting?
- **Honesty:** does the seller call it a reproduction, and keep the original clearly separate?

If the answers are pigment, archival paper, printed to order, high-resolution, and honestly described — you are looking at a real fine-art print, not a poster with a nicer price tag.
