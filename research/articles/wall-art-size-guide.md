Here's the short answer, before the detail: a piece hung over furniture usually looks best at **about two-thirds the width of the furniture below it**, and on a bare wall you want the art to fill roughly **60–75% of the usable width**. In practice, that puts most living-room wall art somewhere between a 16×20-inch print and a 24×36-inch canvas. If you take nothing else from this wall art size guide, size *up* when you're unsure — the most common mistake is going too small.

The rest is about turning that instinct into a measurement you can actually order. Choosing the image is the easy part; this page is only about scale. For the broader question of *which* piece suits a room — colour, mood, light — see the companion article on [how to choose landscape wall art for your living room](/blog/how-to-choose-landscape-wall-art-for-your-living-room). Here we stay on dimensions.

## First, measure the wall — not the art

Start with a tape measure and two numbers: the **width of the wall** (or the wall *section* the art will live on, between a window and a corner, say), and the **width of any furniture** the art will sit above. Those two numbers decide almost everything.

Then do the cheapest, most useful thing in this whole guide: cut a rectangle of newspaper or kraft paper to the size you're considering, tape it to the wall, and live with it for a day. What looks generous on a screen often looks modest on a real wall — which is exactly why people under-size. Seeing the real footprint before you buy prevents nearly every sizing regret.

## How much of the wall should the art fill?

Think in proportions, not absolute centimetres. These are practical guidelines, not laws — but they're the ones that reliably look intentional.

### Above a sofa or a console

Aim for a piece, or an arrangement, about **two-thirds the width of the furniture**. A 210 cm (roughly 84-inch) sofa wants art around 140 cm (about 55 inches) across. Hang it so the **bottom of the frame sits 15–25 cm (6–10 inches) above the sofa back**, so the art and the seating read as one group rather than two things sharing a wall. A horizontal (landscape) image usually settles most naturally here, echoing the line of the sofa — pieces like [Road Through Gold](/prints/road_through_gold) or [Beyond Every Limit](/prints/beyond_every_limit) suit this spot.

### Above a bed or a console table

Same two-thirds proportion, measured against the bed's or console's width. Over a bed, keep the piece centred on the headboard and resist hanging it too high — the centre of the art wants to stay near eye level for someone standing, not float up toward the ceiling. A quieter image such as [Found in Silence](/prints/found_in_silance) works well in a bedroom or above a narrow console, where you want calm rather than a statement.

### A bare wall with no furniture under it

A stairwell, a hallway, a tall lounge wall — here the furniture rule doesn't apply, so you're filling *the wall*. Aim to cover **60–75% of the usable wall width**, leaving clear margins so the piece can breathe. This is where a large format earns its place: an 18×24-inch print, or a 24×36-inch canvas, reads as deliberate. A single strong image like [Rebirth](/prints/rebirth) can hold a wall like this on its own.

**A note on height that ties it together:** whatever the size, the gallery convention is to centre the piece at about **57–60 inches (145–152 cm) from the floor** — average eye level. It's a guideline, but a dependable one.

## Small, medium, or large? Wall art sizes at a glance

It helps to translate "two-thirds of the wall" into the actual wall art sizes you'll be choosing between. Roughly:

- **Small — A3 (30×42 cm) or 12×16 in (30×40 cm):** beside a doorway, a reading nook, a hallway, or as one of a pair.
- **Medium — 16×20 in (40×50 cm):** the dependable middle — holds a modest wall, or sits above a compact sofa or console.
- **Large — 18×24 in (45×60 cm):** a proper focal point on a standard living-room wall.
- **Statement — 24×36 in on canvas (60×90 cm):** a big sofa, or an empty wall that needs filling.

Small pieces aren't wrong — they're just for small jobs, or for grouping. A single small print alone on a wide wall is the classic too-small look.

## One large artwork, or a group?

Both work; they solve different rooms.

- **One large piece** is calmer and more contemporary. It gives the eye a single place to rest, which suits a modern room already busy with texture and furniture. When a wall can take it, this is usually the easier win.
- **A pair or a trio** suits a wide wall, or a person who likes a little rhythm. If you go this way, size the *whole arrangement* — frames plus the gaps between them — to that two-thirds proportion, and keep an even gap of **5–8 cm (2–3 inches)** between frames so they read as one unit.

What rarely reads as intentional is a scatter of tiny frames trying to do the job of one confident piece.

## Portrait or landscape? Match the art to the wall's shape

Orientation is part of sizing, because it decides how the piece uses the space. A simple rule of thumb: **let the art echo the shape of the wall or the furniture.**

- A **wide, low** span — above a sofa, a bed, a sideboard — usually wants a **landscape** (horizontal) piece.
- A **tall, narrow** wall — between two windows, a slim hallway panel, beside a bookshelf — wants a **portrait** (vertical) piece.

Most of Ani's landscapes are horizontal, which is why they sit so comfortably above seating; a tall wall wants a vertical piece instead, or it fills with blank space on either side.

## Paper prints vs canvas — where the scale changes

Paper and canvas mostly differ in *look*, but two things about **size** are worth knowing — a quick canvas size guide within the bigger picture:

- **Canvas reads slightly larger than the same nominal size on paper.** A stretched canvas has depth and no mat or frame border eating into the image, so a 16×20-inch canvas occupies a touch more visual space than a 16×20-inch framed paper print. If you're on the line between two sizes and choosing canvas, you can often take the smaller one.
- **Framing adds to a paper print's footprint.** A mat and frame can add several centimetres all round, so factor the *framed* dimensions into your two-thirds maths, not just the printed image.

Beyond that, it's taste — paper reads a little more like a fine-art print, canvas a little more like a painting. The material trade-offs in depth are covered in the [living-room wall art guide](/blog/how-to-choose-landscape-wall-art-for-your-living-room).

## The mistake that makes art look too small

Almost every sizing regret is the same one: under-sizing. A small frame lost above a three-seat sofa reads as an afterthought no matter how good the image is. When you're between two sizes, choose the larger — and use the paper-on-the-wall test first, so "larger" is a decision you've *seen*, not a gamble.

## Frequently asked questions

**What size should wall art be above a sofa?**

Aim for a piece — or a group — about two-thirds the width of the sofa. For a 210 cm (84-inch) sofa that's roughly 140 cm (55 inches) across. Hang it so the bottom of the frame sits about 15–25 cm above the sofa back, with the centre near eye level.

**How much of the wall should artwork cover?**

On a bare wall, aim to fill about 60–75% of the usable width, leaving clear margins. Over furniture, size to the furniture (about two-thirds its width) rather than to the whole wall.

**Is one large piece better than a group of smaller ones?**

For most modern rooms, a single larger piece feels calmer and more intentional. Choose a pair or trio only if the wall is wide or you want rhythm — and size the whole arrangement, gaps included, by the two-thirds guideline.

**What are the most useful wall art sizes for a living room?**

16×20 in and 18×24 in are the dependable middle for a standard wall, with a 24×36-inch canvas for a large sofa or an empty wall, and A3 or 12×16 in for smaller spots and pairs.

**Should I choose a paper print or a canvas?**

It's mostly a look — paper reads more like a fine-art print, canvas more like a painting. For scale, remember a canvas reads slightly larger than the same nominal paper size, and a framed paper print's mat and frame add to its footprint.
