Most living rooms end up with art almost by accident — something that was on sale, at roughly the right size, in roughly the right colour. It works, sort of. But the rooms that feel considered usually got there by answering three questions on purpose: how big, how high, and what mood. None of them require a degree in interior design.

Here is how I think about it, as a painter whose work ends up on other people's walls.

## Start with size — most art is hung too small

The single most common mistake is a piece that is too small for its wall. A stamp of a picture floating over a large sofa reads as an afterthought.

A simple rule that rarely fails: **a piece (or a group) above a sofa should span roughly two-thirds of the sofa's width.** Over a three-seat sofa, that means a genuinely large piece — which is exactly why searches for [large wall art for living room](/prints) are so common. If a single work that size is out of reach, two or three related pieces hung close together read as one large statement and solve the same problem.

When in doubt, size up. A room forgives art that is slightly too big far more easily than art that is too small.

## Hang it at eye level, not ceiling level

The second mistake is height. Art drifts upward — toward the picture rail, toward the ceiling — and ends up in a no-man's-land above where anyone actually looks.

The gallery convention is to centre a piece so its **middle sits about 145–150 cm (57–60 in) from the floor** — roughly average eye level. Above a sofa, leave a hand's-width to a forearm's-length of breathing room between the top of the sofa and the bottom of the frame, and keep the centre near that same eye line. Hung there, a painting belongs to the room rather than hovering over it.

## Choose the mood before the colour

Only now does colour matter — and it matters less literally than people think. You are not matching a cushion. You are setting a **mood**.

A living room is where a household actually decompresses, so most rooms are served by art that lowers the temperature rather than raises it: open horizons, soft light, water, sky. That is why [calming landscape wall art](/artworks) and coastal, blue-toned pieces work so reliably in living spaces — they give the eye somewhere to rest.

A blue seascape like this one, for instance, does more for a room's sense of calm than any amount of colour-matching:

The test is simple: imagine the piece on the wall at the end of a long day. Does it make the room feel calmer and more open, or busier? Choose calmer.

## Paper, canvas, or an original?

Once you know the size and the mood, the format is a practical choice:

- **A fine-art print on archival paper**, framed, gives a crisp, contemporary, gallery feel.
- **A canvas print** reads warmer and more like a painting on the wall, and needs no frame.
- **An original painting** is the one-of-a-kind object — the piece no one else will ever own.

All three can be the right answer; it depends on the wall, the budget, and how much you want the surface itself to feel like a painting. I've written a separate, plain-language guide to the print side of that decision: [giclée prints vs standard art prints](/blog/giclee-prints-vs-standard-art-prints).

## A short, unglamorous checklist

Before you commit to a piece for a living room:

- **Measure the wall and the sofa.** Aim for ~two-thirds of the sofa's width.
- **Mark the centre at ~145–150 cm** from the floor before you hang anything.
- **Pick the mood first** — calmer almost always wins in a living room.
- **Then pick the format** — paper, canvas, or original — to suit the wall and budget.
- **Buy slightly bigger than feels safe.** You will not regret it.

That's genuinely most of it. Get the size and height right, choose a piece that makes the room feel calmer, and the "does this look intentional?" question answers itself.
